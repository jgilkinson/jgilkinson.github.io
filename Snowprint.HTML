Using Snowprints to Track Bears in the Arctic

Project Snowprint explores whether multilocus environmental DNA can recover individual identities and kinship in elusive polar bear populations — without tags, collars, or direct handling.

Methods
Genotyping

Field-style samples were stripped of identity metadata and reduced to collection info plus multilocus genotypes. Alleles were concatenated into a unique wild_id per sample, then compared using Hamming distance to infer potential parent–offspring and close-kin ties.

  • Blind multilocus genotyping (no pre-existing tags)
  • Pairwise Hamming distances for kinship
  • PCA to reveal population structure and diversity

Key Findings
Analysis

The eDNA-only model overestimates individual counts but accurately recovers kinship structure. Almost every inferred bear participates in at least one breeding cluster, revealing a dense, genetically diverse population.

  • Model: 2,127 inferred bears vs. 1,666 true individuals
  • 2,112 bears in breeding clusters; just 15 unconnected
  • Clear individualization, limited evidence of sub-population splits
Ethics & Impact
Conservation

Project Snowprint prioritizes welfare: no animals are trapped, collared, or sedated. eDNA expands the reach of wildlife monitoring while reducing field risk, making high-resolution genetics accessible to more conservation teams.

Population & Kinship from Snowprints Alone

Starting from 3,385 environmental samples, Project Snowprint demonstrates that multilocus eDNA can reconstruct both population size and kinship networks at high resolution. The current workflow tends to over-split individuals, but offers near-complete coverage of breeding bears and a strong foundation for next-generation monitoring pipelines.
